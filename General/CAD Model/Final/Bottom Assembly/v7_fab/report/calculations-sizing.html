Calculations & Sizing

This page presents the engineering calculations that size the height-adjustment subsystem: from the payload and service factor to the screw-jack selection, linear speed requirement, and motor input specification.

Payload & Service Factor

The estimated payload — the mass of everything above the height-adjustment column — is approximately 10 kg. This includes the torso frame, arms, padding, sensors, and mounting hardware.

Wpayload = m × g = 10 kg × 9.81 m/s² ≈ 98.1 N

A light-shock service factor of 1.5 is applied to account for:

  • Minor bumps and transients during height adjustment in demo environments
  • Uncertainty in actual mass distribution after final assembly
  • Dynamic amplification from start/stop acceleration of the screw jack

Fdesign = Wpayload × SF = 98.1 N × 1.5 ≈ 147 N

This is the design axial load for screw-jack selection. Lateral loads from punching are carried by the telescopic column, not the screw jack.

Stroke & Speed Requirement

The required vertical stroke is 400 mm, covering the head and torso target zone repositioning across the intended user height range.

The engineering target for adjustment time is approximately 32 seconds over the full 400 mm stroke. This yields a required linear speed:

vlinear = 400 mm / 32 s = 12.5 mm/s

Equivalent to approximately 0.75 m/min — consistent with typical screw-jack output speeds at moderate motor RPM.

This speed is practically sufficient for a setup-time operation. Users adjust height between training sessions, not during rounds, so a 32-second full-stroke time is acceptable. An aspirational user-preference target of ~10 seconds has been noted for future motor-control optimisation.

Screw Jack Selection: HK2T

The HK2T travelling-nut screw jack was selected as the axial lifting device. In a travelling-nut configuration, the screw rotates while the nut moves linearly — the nut is connected to the moving inner member of the telescopic column.

Model HK2T

Design load ~147 N (including 1.5× service factor)

Motor Input Requirement

Based on the screw-jack lead and the required linear speed of 12.5 mm/s, the back-calculated motor speed requirement at the jack input shaft is approximately:

nmotor ≈ 800 rpm

This is the input speed required at the screw-jack worm gear shaft to produce the target 12.5 mm/s output. The actual value depends on the jack's internal gear ratio and screw lead.

At 800 rpm, the motor torque requirement is modest because the screw-jack's internal worm gear provides significant torque multiplication. The motor-sizing logic therefore focuses on speed capability rather than torque.

Self-Locking Behaviour & Safety

The self-locking nature of the screw jack provides inherent fail-safe behaviour:

  • The column holds its position even without motor power — if the motor is de-energised or loses power during training, the robot remains at its set height.
  • No external brake or locking mechanism is required, reducing complexity and eliminating potential brake-failure scenarios.
  • The self-locking property also means the column does not bounce or oscillate under punching loads — the screw cannot be back-driven by axial impact forces.

This fail-safe characteristic was a significant factor in the selection of a screw jack over powered alternatives (electric actuators, pneumatic cylinders) that would require active holding to maintain position.

Requirement Traceability

The calculations on this page verify that the mechanical design meets system-level requirement RM-5 (Height Adjustability): the robot must accommodate users of different heights by providing a vertical adjustment range of at least 400 mm within an acceptable setup time.

  • The HK2T screw jack is rated well above the 147 N design load, providing adequate margin for the 10 kg payload plus service factor.
  • The self-locking property eliminates the need for external brakes or holding mechanisms, satisfying fail-safe requirements without additional hardware.
  • The 12.5 mm/s linear speed at 800 rpm motor input delivers the full 400 mm stroke within 32 seconds, meeting the engineering target for setup-time operations.

The corresponding verification method — loaded stroke test with timing and positional accuracy measurement — is described in the Robot Mechanism verification plan. Electrical motor integration and speed control are documented separately by the upper-assembly agent.
