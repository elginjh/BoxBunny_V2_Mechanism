Lift vs Structure Separation

The defining design principle of BoxBunny's height-adjustment subsystem is the separation of axial lifting from lateral structural support. This page explains the load-path logic behind this decision and the evolution from the earlier dual linear-guide concept.

The Problem: Combined Loading

The height-adjustment column sits directly within a major load path beneath the upper body. During operation, it experiences two fundamentally different types of loading:

  1. Axial (vertical) load: The weight of everything above the column — torso frame, arms, padding, sensors — acts downward through the column. This is a steady, predictable load.
  2. Lateral loads and overturning moments: Punches create horizontal forces at the torso/head height. These forces are transmitted through the column as lateral shear and bending/overturning moments. These loads are dynamic, impulsive, and variable in direction.

If a single mechanism is required to handle both load types simultaneously — as in a typical linear actuator — the design becomes significantly more complex, expensive, and prone to failure.

Decoupled Load Paths

The key design decision is to treat the screw jack as purely an axial lifting device, operating mainly in compression. Lateral forces and overturning moments — especially those from punching — are resisted by the surrounding telescopic column structure.

Load Type Carried By Rationale
Axial (weight) Screw jack Screw jacks excel at carrying compressive axial loads; self-locking behaviour provides inherent position hold
Lateral (punch forces) Telescopic column Steel tube-in-tube construction with Delrin bearings provides high lateral stiffness and moment resistance
Overturning moment Telescopic column Bearing surfaces at top and bottom of travel provide a wide moment arm to resist tilting

Why This Matters

This separation prevents side-loading of the screw jack, which would otherwise:

  • Reduce screw-jack efficiency (increased friction from lateral contact)
  • Accelerate wear on the screw and nut surfaces
  • Create binding risk — the nut may jam on the screw if lateral forces push the components out of alignment
  • Require a more expensive screw jack rated for combined loading

By routing lateral loads around the jack and through the surrounding column, the screw jack operates within its intended load envelope (axial compression only), maximising efficiency and minimising wear.

Evolution from Dual Linear-Guide Concept

An earlier concept used two vertical linear rails at the rear with a lift carriage plate. The screw jack was positioned near the centreline, with a compliant interface to tolerate misalignment between the jack's output and the rail-guided carriage.

This concept established the correct engineering principle — separating lift from lateral support. However, it introduced practical issues:

  • Too many precision-mounted components: Two linear rails, four bearing blocks, a carriage plate, and a compliant coupling between jack and carriage.
  • Tolerance sensitivity: The two rails must be perfectly parallel. Any misalignment creates binding or uneven block loading, which is difficult to achieve in a welded steel structure.
  • Manufacturing complexity: Machined flat mounting surfaces required for each rail on a welded structure.

The project moved toward a custom telescopic column using SHS-based steel structure, Delrin bearing interfaces, and a modular 8080 aluminium inner member. This consolidated the guiding function into a single structural unit, eliminating the rail parallelism problem and reducing the number of precision components.

The "Guided Structural Mast" Analogy

The final concept behaves less like an exposed actuator and more like a guided structural mast. The outer welded tube provides the structural envelope; the inner 8080 extrusion slides within it on Delrin wear pads; and the screw jack operates inside the column, hidden from view and protected from external disturbances.

This arrangement also provides a clean aesthetic — the mechanism is enclosed rather than exposed, which is important for a robot that operates in close proximity to users and must project a professional, safe appearance.
