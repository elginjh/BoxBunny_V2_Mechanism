Concept Generation & Selection

This page documents the three height-adjustment concepts evaluated for BoxBunny to satisfy system-level requirement RM-5 (Height Adjustability), the rejection rationale for each alternative, and the selection of the final manual screw jack with vertical linear guides architecture.

Design Constraints

The height adjustment system must vary the torso/head height by approximately 300–400 mm to accommodate different user heights, while:

  • Carrying the axial load of the upper structure (~20–25 kg including arms and padding)
  • Resisting lateral forces and overturning moments from punches
  • Remaining stiff and "non-bouncy" during training — once set, the height should not oscillate
  • Being safe and simple for users to adjust occasionally (not continuously during a round)
  • Fitting within the available vertical envelope above the linear stage

Concept #1: Office Chair Mechanism + Vertical Linear Guide

The first concept adapted the office chair mechanism: a gas lift cylinder to carry the axial load, combined with vertical linear guides at the back to resist lateral punching forces.

Advantages

  • Gas lifts are readily available and compact
  • Can carry the static axial load of the robot mass
  • Vertical linear guides at the back can handle lateral loads and overturning moments

Rejection Rationale

  • Gas cylinders are inherently compliant — they behave like a spring/damper. Under repeated punches, this leads to vertical "bouncing" of the torso, which feels unrealistic and reduces strike rigidity.
  • The internal gas spring force varies with stroke; stiffness is not easily tuned for the specific mass and dynamic loads.
  • Office-chair gas lifts are designed for human seating comfort, not for resisting repeated impact loads at an elevated lever arm.

Conclusion: Insufficient vertical stiffness — the gas lift introduces unwanted bounce under impact.

Concept #2: Electric Gas Strut + Motorised Vertical Linear Guide

The second concept combined an electrically actuated mechanism for height control with a gas strut at the bottom to assist in carrying axial load:

  • A motorised drive (screw or belt) to move the upper carriage along vertical linear guides
  • A gas strut mounted beneath to partially support the robot's weight, reducing motor torque

Advantages

  • Allows push-button height adjustment, potentially even mid-session
  • The gas strut can reduce motor torque by counterbalancing part of the robot's weight
  • Vertical linear guides again provide lateral and moment support

Rejection Rationale

  • Mechanically and electrically complex: coordinating motor drive, gas strut support, and guide loads is non-trivial.
  • Multiple elements share the load path (motor, screw/drive, gas strut, guides), making stiffness and reliability harder to predict.
  • For an application where height is adjusted occasionally (between users, not continuously), the added complexity and cost of powered adjustment is not justified.

Conclusion: Unnecessary complexity — high cost for an infrequent adjustment.

Concept #3: Manual Screw Jack + Vertical Linear Guides (Selected)

The final concept uses a manual screw jack mounted on top of the linear stage, combined with vertical linear rails as primary guides:

  • The screw jack carries the axial load and provides precise height adjustment
  • Two vertical linear rails (each with multiple long blocks) carry lateral forces and overturning moments from punches

Why This Concept Was Selected

Property Benefit
High axial stiffness Screw jacks are designed for heavy-duty industrial applications; minimal vertical compliance
Self-locking Non-backdrivable screw — height does not drift or bounce under impacts; no extra brake required
Simple load path Axial loads go directly through the screw jack; lateral loads go through the rails → easier to analyse
Manual but ergonomic Acceptable because height changes occur between users/rounds, not continuously
Robust and maintainable Standard industrial components (jack + linear guides) with proven reliability

Trade-offs

  • Requires manual effort to adjust height; not as convenient as push-button systems
  • Adjustment speed is slower than an electric actuator, though still acceptable for occasional use

Evolution to Custom Telescopic Column

Although Concept #3 established the correct engineering principle (screw jack for axial load, separate guides for lateral loads), the original implementation using two rear-mounted linear rails introduced too many precision-mounted components and tolerance sensitivity. The design evolved toward a custom telescopic column that consolidates the guiding function into a single structural unit — documented further in the Lift vs Structure Separation and Structural Layout & Fabrication pages.

Commercial off-the-shelf telescopic columns were also considered but rejected because their side-load capability under boxing disturbances was uncertain and vendor specifications did not address impact-type loading.
